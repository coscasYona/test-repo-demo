# Program Overview & Science

## Your Starting Point

You have a **strong strength base** (130 kg squat, 180 kg deadlift) but a **beginner-to-intermediate running base** (~5.25 km in 30 min). This is actually an advantage — your musculoskeletal system is already resilient, which means you can handle running volume increases better than someone with no strength background.

The key challenge: **building aerobic endurance from ~5 km to 42.2 km in 20 weeks while simultaneously losing body fat and preserving muscle mass.**

## The Science Behind This Program

### 1. Polarized Training Model (Stephen Seiler, 2010)

Research on elite endurance athletes consistently shows that **~80% of training should be at low intensity** (conversational pace, Zone 1-2) and **~20% at high intensity** (tempo, intervals). This approach:

- Builds mitochondrial density and capillary networks
- Improves fat oxidation (critical for both marathon fueling and getting lean)
- Minimizes injury risk from excessive hard running
- Allows adequate recovery for concurrent strength training

> **Reference:** Seiler, S. (2010). "What is best practice for training intensity and duration distribution in endurance athletes?" *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*, 5(3), 276-291.

### 2. Zone 2 Training & Metabolic Efficiency (Iñigo San Millán)

Zone 2 training (where you can hold a conversation but feel you're working) specifically targets **fat oxidation capacity**. This is where your body learns to burn fat as fuel — essential for both:

- **Marathon performance:** You can't carry enough glycogen for 42 km; you need to burn fat efficiently
- **Getting shredded:** Higher fat oxidation rate = better body composition over time

> **Reference:** San-Millán, I., & Brooks, G. A. (2018). "Assessment of metabolic flexibility by means of measuring blood lactate, fat, and carbohydrate oxidation responses to exercise in professional endurance athletes and less-fit individuals." *Sports Medicine*, 48(2), 467-479.

### 3. Concurrent Training (Hickson, 1980; Wilson et al., 2012)

Running high volume while lifting heavy can create an "interference effect." We manage this by:

- **Reducing strength volume** (not intensity) as running volume increases
- **Prioritizing compound lifts** — squat, deadlift, bench remain but with fewer sets
- **Separating hard sessions** — never do a hard run and heavy lift on the same day
- **Keeping strength intensity high** — you maintain strength by lifting heavy for fewer reps, not by lifting light for many reps

> **Reference:** Wilson, J. M., et al. (2012). "Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises." *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*, 26(8), 2293-2307.

### 4. Protein Distribution & Lean Mass Preservation (Norton & Layman, 2006)

When in a caloric deficit, protein becomes critical. The research shows:

- **1.8–2.2 g/kg/day** of protein to preserve lean mass during a deficit
- **Distribute across 4–5 meals** (0.4–0.5 g/kg per meal) to maximize muscle protein synthesis
- **Include protein before sleep** (casein or cottage cheese) — shown to improve overnight recovery

> **Reference:** Morton, R. W., et al. (2018). "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." *British Journal of Sports Medicine*, 52(6), 376-384.

### 5. Carbohydrate Periodization (Jeukendrup, 2017)

Not all training days need the same nutrition. We use **fuel for the work required:**

- **High-carb days:** Long runs and hard intervals — 5–7 g/kg carbs
- **Moderate-carb days:** Easy runs and moderate gym — 3–4 g/kg carbs
- **Lower-carb days:** Rest days — 2–3 g/kg carbs

This creates a natural caloric cycling that supports performance when needed and promotes fat loss on easier days.

> **Reference:** Impey, S. G., et al. (2018). "Fuel for the work required: a theoretical framework for carbohydrate periodization and the glycogen threshold hypothesis." *Sports Medicine*, 48(5), 1031-1048.

## Your Training Zones

Based on your current 10.5 km/h (5:43/km) pace for 30 minutes, here are your estimated training zones:

| Zone | Name | Pace (min/km) | Heart Rate | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Zone 1 | Recovery | 7:30–8:00 | < 65% max | Very easy, could chat freely |
| Zone 2 | Easy/Aerobic | 6:30–7:15 | 65–75% max | Conversational, comfortable |
| Zone 3 | Tempo | 5:45–6:15 | 75–85% max | Comfortably hard, short sentences |
| Zone 4 | Threshold | 5:15–5:40 | 85–90% max | Hard, few words at a time |
| Zone 5 | VO2max | 4:45–5:10 | 90–95% max | Very hard, can't talk |

**Important:** These paces will improve as your fitness develops. Re-test every 4 weeks by doing a comfortable 30-minute run and noting your average pace.

## Five Training Phases

| Phase | Weeks | Focus | Weekly Running Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Base Building | 1–4 | Build running habit, aerobic foundation | 15–25 km |
| 2 — Aerobic Development | 5–8 | Extend long run, introduce tempo | 25–35 km |
| 3 — Endurance Building | 9–12 | Long runs to half-marathon distance | 35–50 km |
| 4 — Marathon Specific | 13–17 | Race-pace work, peak volume | 50–65 km |
| 5 — Taper & Race | 18–20 | Reduce volume, sharpen, race day | 65→30 km |

## Expected Timeline

- **Weeks 1–8:** You'll feel your running improve rapidly. Body composition changes begin.
- **Weeks 9–12:** Long runs become the centerpiece. Noticeable leanness.
- **Weeks 13–17:** Peak fitness. You'll look and feel shredded.
- **Weeks 18–20:** Taper — you'll feel restless but trust the process.
- **Race Day:** You run 42.195 km. You earned it.
